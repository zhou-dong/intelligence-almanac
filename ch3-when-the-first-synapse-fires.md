# Facts — When the First Synapse Fires (Chapter 3)

*This file holds facts specific to this chapter. Extracted and expanded from material originally folded into `ch4.1-when-many-cells-move-as-one.md § Cnidarian ("radiatan") nervous system` on 2026-09-21, once cnidarians got their own chapter number instead of being background context for the bilaterian chapter.*

## Overview

This chapter follows a generic cnidarian (the lineage including corals, sea anemones, and jellyfish), branching off after sponges (chapter 2) but before bilaterians (chapter 4). Cnidarians are the first lineage in this story with **real neurons and real chemical synapses** — a genuine computational substrate that sponges (chapter 2) entirely lack. Their nervous system is a **nerve net**: neurons distributed through the body with no central integration point, so a stimulus at one location propagates outward through the net rather than converging anywhere. Despite having no convergence point at all, cnidarians already show documented experience-based synaptic plasticity (habituation) — meaning tunable, experience-modifiable synapses arrive *before* centralization does, not after. This chapter is the point in the story where a "weight" (a synapse's strength) first exists as a real, tunable, biological thing, even though there's nowhere yet for many weighted inputs to converge into one decision — that arrives with bilaterians in chapter 4.

## Nervous system structure

- Cnidarians possess a **nerve net**: neurons distributed through the body with no central integration point. Stimulation at one location propagates outward through the net; there is no single site where signals from the whole body converge into one output. (established)
- Cnidarians already have real chemical synapses between neurons, with variable transmission strength between different synapses. (established)
- Cnidarian nervous systems use both classical small-molecule transmitters (glutamate, GABA, serotonin, catecholamines) and, especially prominently, neuropeptides — the specific transmitter identity involved likely varies by species and circuit. (plausible — general characterization; species/circuit-level specifics vary)
- Consequence for the story's throughline: the *tunable synapse* (a weight sitting in a connection, adjustable independently of the sensor) predates bilaterians and is not a bilaterian innovation — it belongs to this chapter. What bilaterians add later (chapter 4) is **centralization**, a specific place where signals from across the body converge into one decision, not the synapse itself. (established, given the structural facts above)

## Experience-based plasticity — habituation

- Cnidarians are the earliest-branching lineage in this tree with synapse-weight-updating-from-experience documented, because they're the earliest-branching lineage with neurons at all — sponges (chapter 2) split off first and have no neurons or synapses, so the question doesn't even apply to them. **Habituation** (a synapse weakening its response purely from the animal's own repeated stimulation, no target or comparison involved) is documented in cnidarians. (established for cnidarians specifically; "earliest lineage where this is even possible" follows from the branching order in `ch4.1-when-many-cells-move-as-one.md § Branching order among animal lineages`, not a separately dated fact — synaptic biochemistry leaves no fossil trace)
- **Mechanistic depth:** the leading candidate mechanism for cnidarian habituation is **synaptic depression** — repeated stimulation reduces transmitter release or postsynaptic sensitivity at the same synapse (a *homosynaptic* effect, confined to the stimulated pathway rather than the whole network). (plausible — synaptic depression is the general mechanism proposed for habituation across many animal groups, and cnidarians have the relevant transmitter machinery, but the specific synapse-level mechanism has **not** been dissected in cnidarians with the same rigor as the comparison case below)
- **Contrast with *Aplysia* (`ch4.2-when-weights-learn.md`):** in *Aplysia*'s gill-withdrawal habituation, Kandel's group traced the mechanism to a *specific*, well-characterized cellular event — reduced calcium influx at the presynaptic sensory-neuron terminal, causing less neurotransmitter release onto the motor neuron. That is established at the single-synapse level, for that one organism. No cnidarian study has pinned down an equivalently specific mechanism — so "cnidarians show habituation" is established, but "cnidarian habituation works via reduced presynaptic calcium influx" would be borrowing *Aplysia*'s mechanism by analogy, not a documented cnidarian-specific finding. Keep these two confidence levels distinct in narration.
- **Convergence and experience-based plasticity are independent capabilities, and plasticity is the older of the two.** A perceptron-style structure (chapter 4's ganglion) is about *wiring* — gathering many inputs into one place before producing an output. Learning-from-experience is about a *single synapse* changing its own strength based on its own recent activity, which needs no convergence point at all: cnidarian nerve nets have no central ganglion anywhere, yet already show documented experience-based synaptic plasticity. So the historical order is plausibly the reverse of what a "structure first, then learning" narrative might suggest — tunable, experience-modifiable synapses came first (this chapter), and centralization arrived later (chapter 4), initially riding on *fixed* weights rather than introducing plasticity itself. (plausible — a structural inference from the evidence above, not a separately documented fact)

## What cnidarians still lack

- No centralization: there is no single site anywhere in a cnidarian's nerve net where many inputs are gathered, weighted, summed, and thresholded into one decision. Every point in the net is structurally similar to every other; nothing plays the role chapter 4's ganglion will play. (established, per the nerve-net structure above)
- Because there is no convergence point, there is nothing yet resembling ch4.1's perceptron-style mapping (weighted sum + bias + threshold) — that specific structural comparison only becomes applicable once a ganglion exists. This chapter's synapses are real and tunable, but each one acts locally within the net, not as one input among many feeding a shared decision. (established, following from the absence of centralization)

## Math model

- Ch2's sponges had no formula at all — no synapses, no weights, nothing to model (`ch2-when-there-is-no-one-home.md § Math model`). This chapter introduces the first thing in the story that behaves like a **tunable parameter**: a synapse's transmission strength, which changes with the animal's own stimulation history (habituation). There is no single named formula for this in the underlying biology comparable to ch1's Michaelis–Menten model or ch4's perceptron rule; the general shape proposed (synaptic depression) is a *decrease* in effective transmission strength as a function of recent repeated activity, roughly `strength ≈ strength − (decay term dependent on recent stimulation)` — stated here only as a qualitative shape, not a sourced formula fit to cnidarian data.
- What's genuinely new relative to ch1 and ch2: a **tunable weight exists as a real biological property of a specific connection**, changeable within an individual's lifetime — something neither ch0's single reaction curve nor ch1's fixed receptor weights nor ch2's uniform calcium-wave propagation have. What's still missing, and doesn't arrive until chapter 4: any place where multiple such weighted connections are gathered and combined into one shared output. (plausible pedagogical framing, following directly from the structural facts above)

## Framing notes

- **Ordering matters for the story's throughline:** this chapter establishes that "has a tunable weight" and "has a convergence point" are two separate, independently-arriving capabilities, and that the tunable weight came first. Chapter 4 should not be framed as "where weights first become tunable" — they already are, here. Chapter 4's actual contribution is centralization. (framing, restating and preserving a distinction already established in `ch4.1-when-many-cells-move-as-one.md § Nervous cluster (ganglion) and the perceptron mapping`)
- Cnidarian descendants alive today include corals, sea anemones, and jellyfish — a full, successful, diversified lineage, not a rung on a ladder toward bilaterians. (established; see the tree-not-ladder framing in `ch4.1-when-many-cells-move-as-one.md § Branching order among animal lineages`)
